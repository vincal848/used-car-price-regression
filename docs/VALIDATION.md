Everything below runs on `data.make_synthetic_used_cars(n=1000, seed=0)`: the original `UsedCarData.xlsx` is not redistributable and is not in this repository, so the synthetic data -- built from known coefficients -- is what pins the modeling code.

## No intercept forces the fit through the origin

The original ran every one of its seven regressions as `sm.OLS(df_car.Price, df_car[[...]])` with no constant column, which fits `Price = b1 x1 + ... + bk xk` with no intercept term at all -- the fit is forced through the origin. Below, the same four predictors (`Age`, `EngineSize`, `Horsepower`, `Doors`) fit with and without a constant, on the same synthetic rows with known coefficients.

| Coefficient | True | No intercept | With intercept |
|---|---|---|---|
| Age | -1400.0 | -957.2 | -1380.2 |
| EngineSize | 2600.0 | 7168.7 | 2450.3 |
| Horsepower | 30.0 | 125.2 | 29.6 |
| Doors | -350.0 | 1190.3 | -450.7 |
| R-squared | -- | 0.9773 (uncentered) | 0.8172 |

Forcing the fit through the origin does not just move the R-squared -- `Horsepower`'s coefficient comes out 125.2 against a true 30.0, and `Doors` flips sign entirely (1190.3 against a true -350.0). The no-intercept R-squared (0.9773) is the *uncentered* figure, `1 - SSE / sum(y^2)`: with an intercept added back, the same data gives 0.8172, because the uncentered number gets credit for `Price` being far from zero, which an intercept already explains for free.
